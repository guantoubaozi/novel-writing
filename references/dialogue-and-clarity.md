# Dialogue, Clarity, and Knowledge Boundaries

## Prepare speaker profiles

Before drafting a scene with dialogue, derive a compact speech profile for every active speaker from confirmed character material and the present situation. Record only distinctions that affect the scene:

- Typical sentence length and completeness.
- Formality, vocabulary, idiom, and technical precision.
- Directness, hesitation, repetition, evasion, humor, and self-correction.
- What changes under pressure, fatigue, intimacy, suspicion, or authority.
- How the speaker talks differently to each important listener.

Do not reduce a character to a catchphrase, accent caricature, or repeated verbal tic. For minor characters, use role, immediate stakes, emotion, and relationship to create two or three concrete distinctions without inventing permanent canon.

## Write spoken language

- Give every line an intention beyond transferring information: obtain, resist, conceal, test, reassure, provoke, delay, save face, or change the relationship.
- Let people speak with imperfect precision when identity and pressure support it. Permit interruption, partial answers, repetition, misunderstanding, correction, and emotional detours.
- Keep military commands and genuine emergencies short when needed. Do not extend the same clipped, exact rhythm into daily conversation, questioning, persuasion, disagreement, or emotional exchange.
- Attach dialogue to physical context often enough that speakers remain embodied in the scene. Do not add gestures mechanically after every line.
- Preserve necessary clarity without making every speaker equally concise, knowledgeable, witty, or correct.

## Test dialogue after drafting

Run these checks on each scene batch:

1. **Name-blind test:** Hide speaker names from a representative exchange. Verify that cadence, word choice, precision, hesitation, humor, or avoidance still distinguish the main speakers.
2. **Information-only test:** Flag exchanges that could be replaced by a factual summary without losing intention, relationship, emotion, or subtext.
3. **Mechanical exchange test:** Flag three or more consecutive turns in which everyone speaks in short, complete, accurate plot-delivery lines unless the scene is a command sequence or immediate emergency.
4. **Pressure test:** Verify that speech changes plausibly with risk, fatigue, status, and relationship rather than preserving one polished register everywhere.
5. **Oral-language test:** Read the exchange as spoken language. Rewrite lines that are grammatical summaries but implausible utterances.

## Keep narration clear

- Establish the actor, action, location, and immediate consequence before compressing their meaning.
- Anchor unfamiliar place names, technical terms, local positions, and pronouns on first use. Do not rely on a spatial relation or referent known only to the author.
- Replace hidden causal leaps with the concrete observation, behavior, or consequence that lets the viewpoint character reach the conclusion.
- Preserve deliberate ambiguity only when the reader is meant to question meaning. Do not confuse ambiguity with missing orientation.
- Make every summary sentence understandable on one reading by an ordinary reader and support it with visible prior evidence.

## Control explanatory and aphoristic prose

Check sentences built around negation, reversal, compressed judgment, or a polished maxim, especially repeated forms such as “not X but Y.” Keep one only when the viewpoint, scene pressure, and preceding evidence earn it.

Rewrite when a sentence:

- Opens with a denial before the reader knows the positive fact.
- Sounds profound while hiding the actor, cause, or consequence.
- States an author-level conclusion unavailable to the viewpoint character.
- Turns a local observation into a universal claim without support.
- Repeats a meaning already shown through action.

Prefer a specific consequence, sensory observation, behavior, or failed action over an abstract verdict. Keep a concise summary when it clarifies a demonstrated pattern rather than replacing the demonstration.

## Enforce knowledge boundaries

Maintain a scene knowledge ledger for consequential information:

- Name the fact or concept.
- Record who knows it, who suspects it, and who does not know it.
- Record how and when each character learned it.
- Separate the author's knowledge, reader knowledge, narrator access, and character belief.

Before a character states, answers, anticipates, or reacts to a concept, identify the line, event, observation, or prior record that made it available. If no source exists, make the character ask, misunderstand, infer visibly, remain silent, or remove the response. Never let a character answer terminology, motives, plans, or history that nobody has told or shown them.
